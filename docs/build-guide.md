# Build guide and decision record

## How to read this guide

This guide reconstructs the Box of Death build from a 91-turn primary conversation plus complete related woodworking, copper-patina, and electrical conversations. `T` references identify primary turns; `W`, `P`, and `E` identify those related sources. The [September 12 follow-up](recent-decisions-2026-09-12.md) adds recent main-chat turns, two branches, projection work, and existing local measurement notes (R1–R4, L1). It records three different kinds of information:

- **Built** means the conversation explicitly says the work was completed.
- **Selected** means the builder accepted the method or design, but completion was not recorded.
- **Explored** means the idea was advice or a render direction and may have been rejected later.

The source conversation contains many generated-image iterations whose images are not embedded here. Recovered visual assets belong in the [image gallery](../images/gallery.html), with machine-readable provenance in `../images/manifest.json`. The source index at the end of this document makes the turn references useful without publishing the private transcript.

The ongoing Apple Note named **“Box of Death”** remains the live project log. It is intended to be copied into the repository at project completion and reconciled with this record. It has not yet been imported.

## 1. Establish the physical design

The design began with a big red button centered in an old carnival box. It developed into a compact tabletop machine with a sloped button deck, upright sign, small perimeter lights, layered facade, scalloped front, and rounded crown. The controlling text is:

> Death the fortune-telling skeleton

> Press
>
> to hear your fortune

The favorite front was the first major render direction. Later image work was required to leave roughly 95 percent of that design unchanged while integrating the correct pirate-skull mark into the crown/backboard. The accepted adjustment lowered the button, enlarged “Press,” and placed “to hear your fortune” below it. (T001–T008, T016–T026)

Use recovered images to resolve shape and decoration. The early numerical elevation was scaled from a generated perspective render and should never override the primed shell. Its estimates are retained in the [project specification](project-specs.md#concept-render-proportions). The recovered T008 graphic disagrees with the T007 text on sign height (175 mm versus 125 mm) and gives 22 degrees where the later build description says roughly 30 degrees. Its ambiguous 105 mm plan-view arrow and raster “Scale 1:5” are not manufacturing references.

Use the [builder-reported measurements](project-specs.md#builder-reported-shell-measurements) for the overall envelope and panel extents. Before further fabrication, verify those rounded values and complete the remaining measurements:

- overall width, height, and depth;

- upright and crown dimensions;
- clamshell seam and hinge clearances;
- facade thickness;
- main button's mounting neck, nut, gasket, bracket, switch, LED holder, total rear depth, and required panel hole;
- intended bulb count, centers, and edge offsets;

The 100 mm button dimension is the outer face diameter. It is not the hole size. The seller supplied no cutout diameter or maximum panel thickness. ## 2. Cut matching profiles and flat shell parts

The design avoids bent thick wood. Most apparent curves come from shaped plywood profiles and thin applied layers.

### Matching parts

For paired side or crown profiles:

1. Lay out one part from a full-size cardboard template.
2. Use a clamped guide for every straight cut.
3. Cut curved and sloped sections slightly outside the line with the jigsaw.
4. Refine the first part with files, a rasp, sanding blocks, and sandpaper wrapped around a suitable form.
5. Trace the finished first part for the second.
6. Cut the second slightly proud.
7. Clamp the pair together and sand/file them as one set.

A circular saw is useful for long guided cuts while the work remains part of a broad, well-supported sheet. The jigsaw handles sloped or irregular profiles. A coping saw can finish tight inside transitions. Do not put a loose, narrow workpiece under a circular saw merely to save time. (T009, T071–T072)

### Wedge and deck

The side profiles establish the box's sloped deck. Internal cleats let flat panels meet without elaborate edge joinery. The source described the deck as “like” 30 degrees in one conversation and the visible button-panel/upright junction as approximately 65 degrees in another. These use different reference planes and neither was measured. (T076, W009)

Where a cleat must match two non-square faces, take the angle from the object:

1. Fit cardboard or thin scrap into the actual corner.
2. Trace both contact planes and trim the template until it seats correctly.
3. Transfer that shape to a safely supported cleat blank.
4. Cut it slightly oversized.
5. File or sand the two bearing faces to a snug fit.

This method captures construction variation better than assuming the nominal design angle. (T076)

The button-panel/upright junction is a prominent visible seam, so the builder rejected visible cleats or fasteners on its face. Historical reinforcement options were edge glue plus three to five hidden 1/2-in-plywood triangular gussets, each roughly 3–4 in along the contact edges, or a full-width angled batten now that an angle-capable circular saw is owned. The side panels also reinforce the junction. No rear reinforcement option was confirmed as installed. Titebond III was recommended for outdoor joints over a photographed LePage carpenter's glue, but the actual glue used is unknown. (W005, W009–W010)

For the back-to-bottom half, one full-width cleat was confirmed glued in place and screws were planned; the half was later reported solid, but screw installation was not explicitly confirmed. Historical recommendations were staggered #6 × 1-in screws through each panel into a 3/4-in cleat, pilot holes, and optional 2.5–3-in triangular end gussets. Those sizes are proposals, not as-built evidence. (W002, W007–W008)

### Layered facade

The front uses a thin applied plywood face, described as approximately 1/8 in, to create the impression of a routed or raised panel. It is a flat lamination rather than a carved recess. Scalloped lips and borders can be cut as separate flat profiles and laminated to the structure in the same way.

The source record conflicts on plywood. An earlier direct build statement says the enclosure was cut from **1/2-in pine plywood** (W001). The later primary conversation identifies **1/4-in plywood for much of the shell, a 1/2-in base, and approximately 1/8-in facade** (T073, T076). These may describe different panels, but the text does not resolve that. Measure and photograph every panel before creating a cut list. Earlier generic recommendations for a mostly 12 mm body and 6/3 mm overlays do not settle the as-built stock. (T009–T013)

### Hinge and closure

The clamshell divides into a back/bottom half and a front/top/side half. The scalloped crown made a top hinge impractical, so the hinge sits at the front/bottom edge. The first arrangement bound; the builder recut the front plate as the complete facade, mounted the hinge on the bottom, and routed it flush. The installed hinge type and screw sizes were not recorded. (W001, W006, W008)

The planned closure uses square-stock cleats in the larger back/bottom shell as registration stops, magnets to pull the moving half against those stops, and a rear leather pull. Stop the cleats short of the hinge; the historical fit allowance was about 0.5–1 mm for paint and seasonal movement. Two to four small embedded neodymium magnets against steel washers or screw heads were suggested, but no size or pull force was selected and installation is not confirmed. Side chains, lid stays, or separate straps were explored as opening-angle limits. (W002, W006)

## 3. Build the barrel crown

The selected low-shop method bridges the front and rear crown profiles with many narrow plywood strips. The builder proposed roughly 1 cm strips cut from 1/4-in plywood. By T078, the crown was assembled and filed smooth; the conversation does not say whether the strips were square or beveled.

### Square-strip method

Square strips form a faceted barrel roof. Fit the inside edge of each strip closely to its neighbor, glue the structure solid, then use wood slivers only in genuinely large gaps. Small outer V-gaps can be filled after cure and blended as one continuous surface.

### Coopered-strip refinement

The later proposal gives each long strip edge a small bevel, making the strip trapezoidal in section. Neighboring edges butt more closely around the curve and reduce filler.

- A convex outside surface needs the visible face wider than the inside face.
- A concave transition needs the visible face narrower than the inside face.
- Reverse the orientation where the curvature reverses.
- Broad curves need less bevel than tight curves.
- Dry-fit several bevel batches rather than forcing a single guessed angle over the entire crown.

The conversation discussed roughly 5–10 degrees per mating edge and gave geometry examples, but the actual crown radius was never measured. Do not treat those numbers as an as-built specification.

Bevel strips while they are still part of wide, securely clamped stock, or use a controlled sanding jig. Do not bevel a loose 10 mm strip with a handheld circular saw. (T076–T077)

Earlier roof options were four to six sections of bendable 1/8-in / roughly 3 mm plywood over front-to-back ribs spaced about 4–6 in apart, kerf-bent 1/4-in plywood, or 1–1.5-in slats. The suggested seams followed natural crests and valleys. These were explored before the later narrow-strip crown was reported complete and should not be described as the method used. (W008)

An even earlier marquee sandwich proposal used 6 mm front and back skins around 19–25 mm spacers; another version used paired 12 mm shaped profiles with thin 3–6 mm overlays. Those exploratory sections are retained as design history and do not resolve the built panel thicknesses. (T009–T013)

## 4. Fit, fill, and sand

The successful shell workflow was:

1. Dry-fit and open/close the clamshell repeatedly.
2. File interference before adding finish thickness.
3. Fill cosmetic seams and nail holes.
4. Sand filler flush.
5. Clean all dust before primer.

The discussed grit sequence was:

- 80–100 grit only for shaping rough cuts, proud filler, or hard glue squeeze-out;
- 120 grit to flatten and blend filler;
- 180 grit for the final wood/filler pass before primer;
- 180–220 grit lightly after primer to remove raised grain, dust nibs, and objectionable brush marks.

The separate LePage filler trial became too stiff and fast-setting to be pleasant for this work. The useful process lesson was to remove only a small working portion, close the container immediately, discard any skin rather than stirring it back in, and leave filler slightly proud for sanding. DAP Premium or WOODPRO-X were discussed as easier paint-grade candidates, but no replacement purchase is recorded. (T069, T073–T074)

## 5. Prime without destroying the fit

The photographed product is **KILZ Original Interior/Exterior Primer**, oil-based, 946 mL, labeled “All-Weather Sealer & Severe Stainblocker.” It was chosen for weather protection and applied with brushes. Recovered label photos specify 1 hour to touch and 2 hours before painting at 25 °C / 50% relative humidity, with oil- or water-based topcoats. The photographed cleanup solvent is **SOLVABLE Professional Grade Acetone, 946 mL**; the primer label lists mineral spirits or acetone for equipment/splash cleanup. See the [photographed product inventory](project-specs.md#products-visible-in-the-recovered-photographs). (T082)

Use the product label as the authority for brush type, thinning, recoat time, cure, cleanup, ventilation, and compatible topcoats. The conversation settled on an oil-compatible natural-bristle or labeled synthetic/blend brush, with a broad brush for panels and a smaller angled brush for the crown, scallops, edges, and openings.

Keep coats thin at:

- the clamshell mating edges;
- hinge and stop surfaces;
- the button and bulb openings;
- any bare-wood recess reserved for later adhesive bonding.

Primer and paint can make a previously smooth clamshell bind. Check the fit after each coating stage. Subtle hand-brushed texture is appropriate; heavy ridges and pools are not the aging effect. (T075, T078–T080)

For any solvent or primer waste, use a compatible, labeled container and follow the current product label, safety data sheet, and local household-hazardous-waste directions. Keep waste solvent separate from motor oil and other chemicals. Do not rely on the historical suggestion of an arbitrary food container or generic solvent volumes. (T081–T083)

## 6. Apply the selected paint history

The exact final color scheme comes from the builder's T086 correction:

1. **Oil-based KILZ primer** — already applied.
2. **Chocolate Swirl (PPU5-18)** — deepest substrate; reads as old wood through the deepest chips.
3. **Almond Butter (MQ2-28)** — final upright/sign color and an older paint generation beneath selected red and blue. Only a sample was purchased, so concentrate it where wear will reveal it.
4. **Berry Brown (PPU24-02)** — black-brown reveal beneath gold or simulated bare-metal areas.
5. **Ocean Abyss (MQ6-01)** — final dark blue/teal for the sloped button deck and monolithic rear.
6. **Red Pepper (PPU2-02)** — final carnival red for the lower front lip, sides, outer marquee band, and other red structural fields.

Let the manufacturer's current recoat and cure directions control the schedule. Build a complete sample stack before committing to the primed prop, especially where oil primer, water-based color coats, wax resist, leaf size, glaze, adhesive, and clear protection meet.

### Wear map

Concentrate wear where handling and impacts make sense:

- outer front corners and edges;
- lower front lip;
- around the main button;
- around bulb sockets;
- marquee perimeter;
- clamshell seam;
- handle or pull;
- a few selected impact points on broad faces.

Leave other areas mostly intact. Uniform peppering looks decorative rather than aged.

### Abrasion and chips

Sanding produces soft abrasion. The historical discussion also explored paraffin/candle-wax resist for abrupt small chips, torn painter's-tape islands for larger losses, and coarse salt for tiny chips. These were recommendations, not completed methods. Test the chosen technique on the full finish sample and stop at Chocolate Swirl rather than cutting through the primer/weather layer.

Do not use petroleum jelly as a casual substitute without a compatibility test; residue can complicate later coatings. Sharp tools can damage the sealed substrate and should not be the default chipping method. (T009, T086–T087)

## 7. Add lettering, leaf, and recessed grime

The sign field remains Almond Butter beneath **“Death the fortune-telling skeleton.”** The deck places **“Press”** above the lowered button and **“to hear your fortune”** below it.

Gold, silver, and copper leaf were already owned. Historical placement ideas were:

- gold on marquee outlines, lettering shadows, fine borders, and raised ornament;
- copper on selected trim, side ornament, button surround, and skull-logo skin;
- silver only in isolated worn areas beneath gold.

A dark ground beneath leaf gives real losses depth. Berry Brown was bought for that role under gold. Dark glaze in lettering, seams, under trim, around bulbs, and in lower corners can suggest accumulated dirt; raised areas should retain the brighter rubbed highlights.

These placements remain design guidance. The real-metal edge decision may reduce the amount of leafed trim. Use each size, leaf, glaze, and sealer according to its current product instructions, and prove compatibility on a sample. (T009–T012, T084–T088)

### Copper-standoff patina

The desired socket finish came from a short discarded copper-pipe piece that naturally aged in a flower pot: irregular brown/black copper with limited green verdigris. The selected intent is **not** to seal the standoffs, allowing outdoor aging to continue. The desired green is concentrated toward the underside of each horizontally mounted ring, with a few irregular runs rather than a uniform turquoise coating. (P001–P002)

The planned experiment was staged soil aging followed by localized salt/vinegar and an ammonia-vapor treatment while the pieces hang in their installed orientation. This is historical design information, not a chemical procedure. Cut, drill, and fit samples before aging. Any chemical treatment must follow the current chemical labels and safety data sheets for ventilation, PPE, compatible containers, separation from other cleaners, handling, and disposal. Test for copper runoff or staining on the finished wood because the deliberate unsealed choice allows continued surface change.

## 8. Make the glass-and-copper marquee lights

The selected visual concept uses real glass globes rather than imitation plastic bulbs. Each LED passes through the facade into a short copper-tube ring that supports a marble/globe.

### Prototype first

Build one complete sample from the actual materials:

1. Measure the copper tube's outside diameter; “1/4 inch” may be nominal rather than actual OD.
2. Measure the globe diameter and contact geometry.
3. Cut a shallow pocket in scrap wood with a matching Forstner bit.
4. Use the Forstner center mark to drill the smaller wire hole.
5. Scuff and clean only the bonding surface of the copper.
6. Fit the LED, ring, and globe with the intended adhesive.
7. Inspect it lit and unlit, in daylight and darkness, from the front and at an angle.
8. Test whether it survives handling and whether a failed LED or globe can be serviced.

The conversation's selected recess was about 1–2 mm: enough to align the ring and resist side loads without hiding half the socket. A 1/4-in Forstner bit was suggested only if it matches the tube's measured OD. (T010–T011, T062–T064)

### Diffusion and bonding

A clear marble may reveal a bright LED point. Cloudy, naturally diffusing, or mechanically frosted glass was suggested for a broader glow. Glass-etching cream appeared in the early brainstorming but should not be a default public method; it introduces corrosive chemical handling that this project has not specified or tested.

For bonding, clear two-part epoxy was proposed for a permanent glass-to-copper joint and neutral-cure clear silicone for a more replaceable joint. The exact products, exterior temperature behavior, and bond area have not been tested. Cyanoacrylate was discouraged because it can bloom on glass and become brittle. Follow the selected adhesive's label and test it on the real glass and copper. (T010, T065)

### Wiring

Bring only the LED through the centered hole and loop excess copper-wire string behind the facade. Keep visible spacing independent from the string's factory spacing. Retain the wire on the hidden face with small, serviceable clips and do not create sharp bends at soldered LED joints.

Verify that the actual three-string grouping can create the desired chase and that its selected state survives or deliberately resets after power cycling. Neither behavior is recorded. (T010, T055, T060)

## 9. Form the copper logo

The planned front emblem is the correct pirate skull, hat, and crossed-sabres design integrated into the top of the backboard. The goal is a shallow, dimensional copper skin that reads like an old foundry plaque.

### Master strategy

The final carving decision was one monolithic hardwood relief master rather than separately carved, fragile sabres, teeth, or hat parts.

- Start from a flat, straight-grained hard-maple blank, historically estimated at 5–6 × 6–8 × 3/4–1 in.
- Attach a paper pattern as a carving guide.
- Rough-cut only the outer profile with the jigsaw.
- Carve the background down while leaving fine features supported by the continuous block.
- Keep the relief shallow, rounded, and free from undercuts so the formed copper can release.
- Keep the back flat and seal/smooth the master so wood grain does not telegraph into the copper.

Earlier layered construction and 25–30 mm projections were exploratory. The later monolithic approach proposed a 20–25 mm blank, a boss 5–8 mm above the recess, and the skull another 3–5 mm above the boss, but these remain estimates. Make a small test relief before committing the logo. The builder has no 3D printer or laser-cutter access; suggestions that depended on them were explicitly rejected. (T032–T044)

### Carving preparation update

The builder owns carving tools but had not yet used them in the September 9 discussion; prior experience was a short sword carved from two 2×4s with a fixed-blade knife. A portable scrap support board was being considered because the waist-height workbench felt awkward. Back-screwing or clamping the blank to a larger board, practicing on scrap, and working from broad relief to details were proposed; neither the support board nor the master was confirmed built. Screw sizes, board dimensions, and example relief depths from that advice are not selected specifications. (R1)

### Copper skin and backfill

The recovered retailer screenshot identifies **K&S 6020 soft-annealed copper foil, 36 gauge, 0.005 in / 0.127 mm, 12 × 30 in** and shows the matching package. Measure the actual stock before fabrication.

Historical discussion proposed pressing or hand-forming the foil over the sealed master with smooth wood, nylon, bone, or polished tools, then removing the shell and backfilling it. Epoxy/resin with a smaller plywood insert was preferred over solid plaster because plaster can be brittle and moisture-sensitive.

Copper annealing with a torch and chemical patinas were discussed, but this guide intentionally does not turn those suggestions into a procedure. If either is used, confirm the foil is bare and coating-free, then follow current product/supplier instructions for the work surface, fire control, ventilation, PPE, cooling, storage, and waste. Never heat adhesive-backed foil or unknown coatings. Follow the patina and sealer safety data sheets and keep treatments away from electrical contacts.

No formed logo or accepted patina sample is recorded. (T032–T039)

## 10. Choose and mount the rear pull

The rear design went through several major revisions:

1. A hidden USB-C service hatch or skull-mouth port was explored.
2. External ports were abandoned when the box became self-contained.
3. A jawless copper skull near the top became a functional clamshell pull, with a finger cup below it.
4. A captured-bolt hardwood core was proposed so the pull could potentially carry the whole box.
5. The ornate skull was disliked against the plain rear.
6. Simpler leather options followed: a vertical loop and a horizontal strap across roughly half the rear, attached with rivet-like brass hardware.

The rear itself is settled: flat, monolithic, one color, no mid-height ridge, no lights, and Ocean Abyss as the final coat. The handle is not settled in the record. The last design family is utilitarian leather with simple visible fasteners. (T030–T031, T040–T054, T066–T068, T086)

If a skull pull is revived, the proposed structural load path was:

```text
hand -> hardwood skull core -> captured bolt heads/studs
     -> internal hardwood backing plate -> box wall
```

The superseded skull-pull concept also explored approximately 12–20 mm projection, 15–20 mm finger clearance, and 50–70 mm width. These were suggested ergonomics, not measurements of a built handle.

Two roughly 1/4-in hex-head machine bolts, wide spacing, large washers, and locknuts were discussed. This was never engineered or load-tested. Describe it as an opening pull unless the complete assembly passes a defined carry test. A leather pull likewise needs a backing plate or other load-spreading attachment appropriate to the actual panel.

## 11. Form the selected copper edge bands

The initial review explored copper and brass instead of Berry Brown simulation. On September 11 the builder explicitly selected copper and bought sheet from Metal Supermarkets for a reported $90, approximately 0.8 mm thick. On September 12 the initial approximate 12 × 36 in description was corrected to **91.5 × 25.5 cm**. Alloy and temper remain unknown. This edge stock is separate from the thin K&S logo foil. (R2–R3)

### Current template and forming decisions

- Construction-paper templates for all required edges are complete and have been taped onto the box. Transfer their fit to the purchased sheet only after allowing for real metal thickness and bends.
- The selected corner-cap direction puts a continuous bend around the visible front corner and overlapping returns underneath, superseding the first L-shaped blank with its visible corner gap.
- The crown and deck front leading edges should have the rounded look of the mockup. A wire/core wrap is selected to try; no core diameter, adhesive, or completed metal prototype is established.
- Paper tore at inside radii and folded at outside radii. The assistant proposed fitting the curved path first, then forming the round edge, with relief cuts on hidden returns and a short inside/outside-radius prototype. This remains untested advice. Suggested 4–5 mm cores are trial sizes, not a manufacturing dimension; determine bend and return allowance from actual stock.
- Visible fastener heads must be real copper for compatible aging and should read as raised rivets. The flat Etsy heads were not the intended look. Round-head solid-copper rivets were suggested, including 3 mm shank × 6 mm length / 5–5.5 mm head and a smaller variant; no purchase, retention method, or patina match was confirmed.
- The visual target is old, grimy, patinated copper, with verdigris explored. The builder rejected metal depicted as flaking paint. The earlier unsealed-socket decision does not automatically settle sealing of all edge trim.

The [recent source record](recent-decisions-2026-09-12.md) preserves exact turn identities and separates selected design from assistant suggestions. Cutting, annealing, forming, bonding, fastening, and patination are not recorded complete.


### Earlier material exploration

Historical advice suggested thin sheet or flashing around 0.3–0.8 mm, with 0.4–0.6 mm / 0.015–0.025 in as a likely working range. The user's initial 2–3 mm thought would be far too heavy for wrapped decorative trim at this scale.

- **Copper:** locally easier to source in the discussion, compatible with the copper bulb sockets, easy to form, and visually coherent.
- **Brass:** potentially more traditional carnival color, but harder to find in the exact thin form.
- **Aluminum:** easy and inexpensive but would need a finish to fit the design.
- **Steel:** considered unnecessarily hard to form and prone to rust for this use.

### Edge method

- Use flat strip bent over wood for curved, scalloped, or changing edges.
- Use thin angle only on straight runs where its leg size and bend radius fit.
- Cut strip widths and lengths from the measured primed shell, not the render.
- Prototype a representative straight corner and a crown curve before cutting the full layout from the purchased sheet.
- Deburr every cut edge.
- Keep fastener points clear of internal wiring and fingers.
- Use adhesive only if it is rated for the chosen metal, wood/paint substrate, and intended exposure.
- Select decorative pins, tacks, screws, or actual rivets based on the structure rather than appearance alone.

Early ideas used 12–20 mm copper straps with escutcheon pins every 25–40 mm, larger copper nails at corners, and only a few real peened rivets. Those dimensions were proposals. The later supplier request instead asked about 3/4–1-in strips. Neither became an installed specification. (T012, T088–T091)

Thin sheet has sharp edges. Forming, cutting, fastening, patination, and dissimilar-metal sealing should follow the selected materials' current instructions and be proven on scrap.

## 12. Mount the electronics

The box contains an ESP32, USB-C battery pack, voltage regulator, three MOSFET modules connected to three LED strings, Wagos, and the main button's switch/LED wiring. Mount all active electronics in the bottom half.

### Removable tray

A removable 1/4-in plywood electronics tray was proposed. A conceptual layout was:

```text
[ battery ]       [ power / switch ]

[ regulator ]     [ ESP32 ]

[ MOSFET 1 ][ MOSFET 2 ][ MOSFET 3 ]

          Wago distribution
```

This is a service layout, not a wiring diagram.

- Boards with mounting holes can use screws and short nonconductive spacing as needed to clear solder joints.
- Boards without holes can slide between small wood rails and be captured by removable edge clips or a crossbar. Do not clamp components.
- Wagos can sit in a shallow wood cradle with a removable retaining bar.
- The battery needs a fitted cradle and a retaining strap.
- Keep the ESP32 antenna away from the battery and metal bands, then test signal quality after final assembly.
- Label every lighting branch and connector.

### Hinge crossing

Bundle the upper-half lighting and button conductors near a rear corner. Provide a loose U-shaped service loop so opening the clamshell distributes flex over a length of wire rather than at a solder joint. Add strain relief on both halves and check the harness through the full range of motion. (T055–T061)

## 13. Wire and implement only from verified hardware

The project is a Matter button trigger for Death. It is not Death's audio or skeleton controller.

The recovered conversation contains no circuit, pin map, firmware, Matter cluster, pairing flow, or event contract. Do not scaffold fictional code to fill that absence.

The apparent preferred power proposal from E001–E003 is shown below. The builder asked whether a buck converter would regulate as intended and the assistant endorsed it; explicit final adoption was not recorded:

```text
5 V USB-C battery/breakout
          +-- ESP32 development board 5V/VIN input
          |
          +-- buck converter adjusted near 3.0 V
                 +-- string 1
                 +-- string 2
                 +-- string 3
```

In this proposal the strings are in parallel, not series. This direction remains conditional: the builder's online value of 2.0–3.3 V was explicitly marked for checking and may be individual LED forward voltage rather than complete-string supply voltage. Preserve any original string controller or resistor box until the topology is known. A resistor-per-string alternative was analyzed with `R = (5 V - V_string) / I_string` and `P = (5 V - V_string) × I_string`, and remained unqualified because string current was unknown. The user did not explicitly reject individual string resistors; one shared resistor cannot create a stable 3 V rail as the switched load changes.

The button lamp has conflicting source descriptions: “up to 15 V” in E001 versus DC 12 V/“up to 12V” in the saved listing. Bench-test the physical polarized insert and power it from a verified rail, not a GPIO.

Before implementation:

1. Identify the exact ESP32, battery pack, regulator, LED strings, Wagos, and MOSFET boards.
2. Measure battery output behavior and whether it shuts down under the always-on lighting load.
3. Identify how each string was originally powered, whether it contains a controller/resistor box, and its LED count; then verify complete-string voltage, steady current, animation current, and polarity.
4. Measure the button switch with a meter; use `COM` and `NO` for the intended normally-open input.
5. Bench-test the separate 12 V button LED insert and its polarity.
6. Characterize MOSFET module topology, common grounds, boot/default state, PWM behavior, current heating, and protection.
7. Select verified rails, regulator settings, wire gauges, switch, connectors, and overcurrent protection.
8. Draw the actual schematic and pin map.
9. Define the Matter interface with the real Death controller: commissioning, trigger semantics, debounce, retries, duplicate handling, offline behavior, and recovery.
10. Test the complete electrical system outside the wooden enclosure before installation.

The MOSFET seller's 5 A claim conflicts with a 1 A field and has no named component or thermal data. Treat the module as uncharacterized until it is measured. The seller's 12 V description applies to the button's LED insert; the switch is a separate dry contact with no published rating. See [project-specs.md](project-specs.md#electronics-and-power) for the recovered hardware evidence.

## 14. Final assembly and test

Once the finish, trim, and electronics are individually proven:

1. Recheck clamshell fit after all coating thickness is present.
2. Install the button using its measured cutout and rear-clearance requirements.
3. Install light sockets and globes from the accepted prototype.
4. Fit edge metal and pull without trapping or puncturing wiring.
5. Mount the electronics tray, battery restraint, Wagos, and hinge harness.
6. Verify polarity and rails before connecting the ESP32 or lights.
7. Test continuous marquee/button illumination and each animated phase. The October 27, 2026 chase selection restores one of three marquee strings lit at a time, advancing every 500 ms, after the builder disliked the October 24 two-at-a-time trial.
8. Test button debounce and Matter triggering repeatedly.
9. Test power-bank runtime and auto-shutoff behavior.
10. Test clamshell opening through repeated service cycles.
11. Test radio performance with the actual metal trim and closed box.
12. Define and test the intended weather exposure; primer alone does not weatherproof the electronics, seams, glass bonds, or battery.
13. Record the finished dimensions, BOM, wiring, firmware identity, finish products, and test results.
14. Copy and reconcile the Apple Note **“Box of Death.”**
